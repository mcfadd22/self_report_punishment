# Conceptual Definitions and References

*Stimulus design reference for the self-report, stated-versus-applied justice, and agent studies*

Each conception is defined by its **behavioral signature**: which factors it says should move a judgment, and which it says should not. These signatures serve as the operational definitions for constructing scenarios, checking coverage, and interpreting results.

---

## 1. Conceptions of punishment

**Retribution (desert).** Punishment is justified by what the offender deserves, which is a function of culpability and, on most views, harm. The view is backward-looking, so forward-looking consequences should be irrelevant to the sanction.

**General deterrence.** Punishment is justified by preventing future offenses by others. On the expected-cost logic, sanctions should rise when detection is less likely, when sentences are publicized, and when the offense is common.

**Specific deterrence.** Punishment aims to prevent this particular offender from reoffending, so it depends on how responsive this person is to sanctions. It overlaps heavily with incapacitation in practice and can be folded into that conception or dropped.

**Incapacitation.** Punishment prevents harm by removing a dangerous person's opportunity to offend. It is driven by recidivism risk.

**Rehabilitation.** Punishment aims to reform the offender. Treatment amenability matters, and the view may favor non-custodial or shorter sentences. Because it predicts a different kind of sanction rather than simply a longer or shorter one, it requires its own response format.

**Expressive (denunciatory).** Punishment communicates societal condemnation. It is sensitive to culpability and harm, and possibly to publicity, which complicates the use of publicity as a deterrence marker.

**Hybrid (limiting retributivism).** Desert sets upper and lower bounds on the sanction, and forward-looking factors operate only within those bounds. This is probably the most common considered view and the one models are most likely to state, so it needs its own behavioral signature (see Section 2).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mental state (purpose → negligence) | ↑ | ? (intentional offenders more deterrable) | — | — | ↑ |
| Harm magnitude | ↑ (? for some) | ↑ | ? | — | ↑ |
| Motive | ? | — | — | — | ? |
| Detection probability (lower) | — | ↑ | — | — | — |
| Publicity of sentence | — | ↑ | — | — | ? |
| Offense base rate | — | ↑ | — | — | — |
| Recidivism risk | — | — | ↑ | — | — |
| Treatment amenability | — | — | — | ↓ custody | — |
| Irrelevant factor | — | — | — | — | — |

### What the matrix implies for design

- **Detection probability** is the only factor uniquely diagnostic of deterrence and should be the primary deterrence manipulation.
- **Publicity** is shared with the expressive view, so it is at most a secondary factor.
- **Harm** is predicted by nearly every view and does not discriminate between them; treat it as a covariate or hold it fixed.
- **Retribution has no unique positive factor.** It is identified by sensitivity to culpability combined with insensitivity to forward-looking factors. Supporting a retributive reading therefore requires demonstrating null effects, which calls for equivalence tests (e.g., TOST) with smallest effects of interest specified in advance and adequate power.
- **The hybrid view predicts an interaction.** Deterrence factors should move sentences in mid-culpability cases but be compressed at the extremes, where desert caps or floors the sentence. Testing this requires cells at both culpability extremes. A model that states a hybrid view but shows uniform deterrence effects, or none, is not behaving like a limiting retributivist.

## 3. Culpability concepts

**Mental states (Model Penal Code hierarchy).** Each vignette must satisfy the definition for its assigned level, which expert raters can verify.

- **Purpose:** the result was the agent's conscious object.
- **Knowledge:** the agent was aware the result was practically certain.
- **Recklessness:** the agent consciously disregarded a substantial and unjustifiable risk.
- **Negligence:** the agent should have been aware of such a risk but was not.

Recklessness and negligence differ on awareness, so the vignette must state explicitly whether the agent considered the risk.

**Harm versus attempt.** Decide whether harm is manipulated independently of mental state, including failed attempts. Whether harm should matter for retributivists is the moral luck question, which links the justice studies to the outcome-bias track.

**Motive.** Folk judgment weights motive heavily, but the Model Penal Code largely does not treat it as an element of culpability. Recommended handling: keep motive as a separate factor and code its predicted role as contested.

---

## 4. Outcome-track concepts

**Decision quality.** The ex-ante merit of a choice given the information available to the agent. It is fixed by the vignette.

**Agent evaluation.** Judgments of the agent's competence and quality of will, meaning the attitude toward others' interests that the choice expressed. Quality of will can be operationalized in three grades:

1. Did not consider the risk.
2. Considered the risk and accepted it.
3. Considered the risk and mitigated it.

**Outcome evaluation.** How bad the result was, independent of anyone's fault.

**Foreseeability versus foresight.** Foreseeability is the probability of harm given the available information, stated explicitly in the vignette. Foresight is what the agent actually anticipated. Hindsight bias concerns foreseeability; quality of will often concerns foresight. Keep them separate in both stimuli and measures.

**Resultant luck.** The only kind of moral luck manipulated. Circumstantial luck (the situation the agent faced) and constitutive luck (the agent's character) are held fixed, and this should be stated in the methods.
